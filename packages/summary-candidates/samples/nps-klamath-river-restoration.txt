The removal of four hydroelectric dams on the Klamath River reopened hundreds of
miles of habitat along the California-Oregon border. Crews dismantled Iron Gate,
Copco No. 1, Copco No. 2, and J.C. Boyle after years of planning by tribes,
agencies, dam owners, conservation groups, and local communities. The project
became the largest dam-removal effort completed in the United States.

The dams had generated electricity, but they did not include fish ladders that
would allow migrating salmon to pass. Their reservoirs also slowed the river,
warmed the water, and supported conditions favorable to harmful algal blooms.
Fish populations declined as access to upstream spawning grounds disappeared and
water-quality problems intensified. For tribes whose cultures and diets are
closely connected to salmon, the losses affected far more than commercial or
recreational fishing.

Dam removal began with preparatory work and reservoir drawdowns. As water levels
fell, landscapes that had been submerged for decades reappeared. Contractors
breached the structures, moved concrete and earth, managed sediment, and
reshaped channels through the former reservoir sites. The work required close
monitoring because sudden changes in water quality or sediment movement could
harm fish and downstream communities.

Restoration teams collected billions of native seeds before the drawdown. They
used those seeds to establish grasses, shrubs, and trees across exposed ground.
Helicopters, machinery, and field crews spread seed and installed plants over
large areas that would otherwise have been vulnerable to erosion and invasive
species. The vegetation is expected to stabilize soil while the river and its
tributaries establish new channels.

The first seasons after removal produced signs that fish were exploring the
reopened watershed. Chinook salmon were documented upstream in Oregon in autumn
2024, returning to water they had been unable to reach for more than six
decades. Biologists are tracking where fish spawn, how juveniles use
tributaries, and whether populations expand over time. Other species, including
steelhead and Pacific lamprey, may also benefit from renewed access and more
natural river conditions.

Removing concrete did not complete the recovery by itself. The Klamath watershed
still faces drought, high temperatures, agricultural demand, habitat loss, and
changing climate conditions. Tributary restoration, wetland recovery, water
management, and long-term monitoring remain necessary. Scientists caution that
salmon populations can fluctuate widely and that rebuilding resilient runs may
take many years.

The project also changed places used for recreation and community access. Former
reservoirs no longer support the same boating patterns, while a free-flowing
river creates different opportunities and hazards. Agencies are updating maps,
visitor information, and safety guidance as shorelines settle and vegetation
grows. Archaeological and cultural resources are being monitored as newly
exposed land changes.

Tribal nations played a central role in advocating for the river and in shaping
restoration goals. Their involvement brought generations of ecological
observation and cultural knowledge into decisions about fish, plants, and water.
Supporters view the project as both an environmental intervention and a step
toward repairing harms caused when the dams blocked an important living
resource.

The Klamath will continue changing as floods move sediment, plants mature, and
fish recolonize habitat. Researchers will compare water temperature, disease
levels, spawning success, and channel form over time. The result will inform
other communities considering whether aging dams still provide enough benefit to
justify their ecological costs.
