The U.S. Department of Agriculture conducts the Farm to School Census to
understand how public school food authorities purchase local products and
connect meals with agriculture education. The census covers activities such as
buying food from nearby producers, serving regional products, operating school
gardens, arranging farm visits, and teaching students where food comes from.
Results help agencies, schools, farmers, and community organizations identify
both progress and remaining barriers.

For the 2023 census, USDA contacted school food authorities across the country
and asked about their work during the 2022-2023 school year. The survey gathered
information on purchasing practices, spending, product categories, partnerships,
and educational activities. It also asked why some districts were not
participating and what kinds of support might help them begin.

Local purchasing can take several forms. A district may buy directly from a
farm, purchase through a food hub, request regional products from a distributor,
or use a vendor that tracks where products were grown. Definitions of local vary
because geography and agricultural production differ by region. A rural district
surrounded by farms faces different conditions than a remote community or a
large urban school system.

Schools reported purchasing fruits, vegetables, dairy products, meat, grains,
and other foods from local or regional sources. Seasonal availability influences
menus, and districts often combine fresh products with frozen or processed items
that can be stored longer. Reliable delivery, food safety requirements, product
specifications, and kitchen capacity all affect what a school can purchase.

Farm to school programs are not limited to procurement. School gardens give
students direct experience with planting and harvesting. Taste tests allow
cafeterias to introduce unfamiliar foods before adding them to regular menus.
Classroom lessons can connect agriculture with science, health, geography,
economics, and local history. Farm visits and producer presentations help
students understand the work behind a meal.

Schools often describe several benefits. Fresh and culturally relevant products
can improve the appeal of meals. Predictable institutional orders can create
markets for farmers and food businesses. Relationships with producers can make
regional supply chains more visible and can support community planning.
Educational activities may also encourage students to try foods they would
otherwise avoid.

The census also documents practical obstacles. Small farms may not produce the
volume or packaging a large district requires. Schools may lack staff time to
identify suppliers, write bids, or verify product origins. Kitchens designed
mainly to reheat prepared food may need equipment and training before handling
raw ingredients. Prices, transportation, seasonal gaps, and complicated
purchasing rules can make local products harder to use consistently.

Federal grants, state programs, nonprofit organizations, extension offices, and
food hubs can help address those barriers. Technical assistance can connect
buyers with producers and translate school requirements into realistic
contracts. Shared purchasing groups can combine demand from several districts.
Investments in cold storage, processing, kitchen equipment, and workforce
training can make local supply relationships more durable.

USDA publishes census data so communities can compare activities and plan future
programs. The results are descriptive rather than a ranking of individual
districts. Because farm to school work depends on local agriculture,
infrastructure, budgets, and student needs, successful programs can look very
different from one place to another. The census is intended to make those
differences visible while showing how school meals can participate in broader
regional food systems.
