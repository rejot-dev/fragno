Astronomers using NASA's James Webb Space Telescope examined the atmosphere of
WASP-39 b, a hot gas giant that circles a star about 700 light-years from Earth.
The planet is roughly as massive as Saturn but follows an orbit much closer to
its star than Mercury does to the Sun. That tight orbit heats the atmosphere to
about 1,600 degrees Fahrenheit and makes the planet a useful target for
transmission spectroscopy.

During a transit, some starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere before
reaching the telescope. Different gases absorb different wavelengths, leaving
patterns that instruments can measure. Webb observed the transit with several
instrument modes covering a broad span of infrared light. Teams then compared
the measurements with atmospheric models to identify the chemicals responsible
for the observed features.

The resulting spectra provided a detailed chemical inventory. Researchers
reported water vapor, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, sodium, potassium, and
sulfur dioxide. The carbon dioxide detection built on an earlier Webb result and
showed how clearly the telescope could identify a molecule important to
planetary climate. The sulfur dioxide finding was especially notable because
scientists concluded that it is produced by photochemistry rather than simply
rising unchanged from deeper layers.

High-energy light from the host star can break apart molecules in the upper
atmosphere. The fragments then participate in reactions that create sulfur
dioxide. Similar light-driven chemistry shapes the atmospheres of planets in the
solar system, including Earth, but it had not previously been established so
clearly for an exoplanet. The result gave researchers a new way to study how
radiation from a star changes the chemistry of a nearby planet.

The observations also offered clues about how WASP-39 b formed. Ratios among
carbon, oxygen, sulfur, and hydrogen can preserve evidence of the materials a
planet collected while it grew. The measured abundances suggest that the planet
accumulated oxygen-rich solids and may have formed farther from its star before
migrating inward. That interpretation connects the present atmosphere with the
planet's earlier history in the protoplanetary disk.

The spectra did not show methane or hydrogen sulfide at the levels some models
had predicted. Those absences also constrain atmospheric chemistry because a
useful spectrum records both what is present and what is missing. Researchers
found signs that clouds are scattered across the planet rather than forming a
single, uniform layer. The combination of multiple instruments helped separate
cloud effects from molecular absorption.

WASP-39 b is not considered habitable, and the project was not a search for
life. Instead, it served as a demanding test of methods that can later be
applied to smaller and cooler worlds. The study demonstrated that Webb can
retrieve several chemical signatures from one atmosphere and that independent
teams can compare results across different observing modes.

Scientists expect future observations to examine more planets with different
temperatures, masses, and host stars. Each atmosphere will add another point of
comparison for models of planet formation, migration, clouds, and chemistry.
Together, those observations are intended to move exoplanet research from
detecting individual molecules toward explaining how whole planetary systems
develop.
