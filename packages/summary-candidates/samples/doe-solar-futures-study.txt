A study from the U.S. Department of Energy examined how solar power could expand
while the country works toward a carbon-free electricity system. The analysis
found that solar energy could provide about 40 percent of U.S. electricity by
2035 and roughly 45 percent by 2050. Reaching those levels would require rapid
construction, major changes to the power grid, and continued investment in
manufacturing and workers.

The study did not assume that solar panels would replace every other source of
electricity. Instead, it modeled solar as part of a broader system that includes
wind, hydropower, nuclear generation, energy storage, transmission, and flexible
demand. A diverse system can supply electricity when sunlight is unavailable and
can respond to seasonal and regional differences. Existing technologies were
sufficient for much of the modeled growth, although further innovation could
lower costs and simplify deployment.

Deployment would need to accelerate substantially. The country would add large
utility-scale solar projects as well as systems on homes, businesses, parking
structures, and other developed land. Rooftop and community solar could broaden
participation, while large projects would deliver much of the total energy.
Decisions about siting would influence transmission needs, land use, wildlife,
agriculture, and relationships with nearby communities.

A grid with much more solar must move electricity across longer distances and
balance changing output throughout the day. New transmission lines could connect
high-quality solar resources with population centers. Batteries and other
storage technologies could shift afternoon electricity into the evening. Grid
operators could also use flexible industrial loads, electric vehicles, building
controls, and improved forecasting to align demand with available power.

Energy efficiency is an important part of the scenario. Efficient buildings and
equipment reduce the amount of new generation needed even as transportation and
heating become more electrified. Electrification can increase total electricity
use while reducing overall fossil-fuel consumption. The study therefore
evaluates solar growth alongside changes in vehicles, buildings, and industry
rather than treating the power sector in isolation.

The transition could support a large workforce. The study estimated that solar
employment could reach between 500,000 and 1.5 million people by 2035. Jobs
would include manufacturing, construction, installation, operations,
engineering, permitting, finance, and grid planning. Training and labor
standards would influence whether communities can access stable careers and
whether projects can be completed quickly enough.

Costs and benefits would not be distributed automatically. Households with
suitable roofs and available capital can adopt solar more easily than renters or
families with low incomes. Community solar, financing programs, consumer
protections, and targeted incentives can help address those differences.
Policymakers would also need to plan for communities and workers affected by
declining fossil-fuel generation.

Domestic manufacturing could reduce supply risks and capture more economic
value. Solar modules are only one part of the system; inverters, racking,
electrical equipment, batteries, software, and construction materials are also
required. Research can improve efficiency, durability, recycling, and the use of
abundant materials. Stronger recycling systems would recover useful materials as
early generations of equipment reach the end of service.

The study presents a technically possible pathway rather than a prediction.
Permitting delays, transmission constraints, supply chains, public acceptance,
financing, and policy changes could all alter the outcome. Its central
conclusion is that solar can become a major source of U.S. electricity without
sacrificing reliability, but only if deployment is coordinated with storage,
transmission, efficiency, and equitable planning.
